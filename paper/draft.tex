\author{Juee Naik, (Riley DeHaan), Michael Kahana}

\newcommand{\abstracttext}{Studies of free recall mostly ask how activity during 
word presentation predicts later memory, while the response to the word itself has received less attention. 
Work on visual word processing with scalp EEG, MEG and fMRI describes a progression from visual to 
language and memory regions, but these methods cannot resolve local activity in both space and time.
Previous intracranial studies have typically recorded from tens of patients with coverage concentrated 
in the ventral visual stream. Here we map the word-onset response across the brain in 362 neurosurgical 
patients performing delayed free recall, comparing each word with the blank screen that preceded it. 
The response was largest in occipital cortex, where alpha (8--13 Hz) power fell and high-gamma 
(70--150 Hz) power rose, and the same signature of engagement appeared, more weakly, in limbic 
and hippocampal regions. Frontal cortex instead showed increased low-frequency and decreased beta power,
 consistent with suppression. High-gamma responses began in lateral occipital cortex (${\sim}$120 ms) 
 and moved forward, reaching orbitofrontal cortex last (${\sim}$300 ms), while alpha synchrony with 
 the rest of the brain increased most in occipital cortex. The occipital response was larger when 
 the word was more expected, after longer waits, and the response weakly predicted later recall, 
 from ${\sim}$200 ms onward and most persistently in frontal cortex. Together, these results show how 
 the response to a word evolves across the brain, from early visual engagement to later frontal 
 involvement, and provide a baseline for interpreting cognitive effects measured during word presentation.}

\section*{Introduction}\label{introduction}

\subsection*{The word-onset response in free recall}\label{intro-word-onset}

Most intracranial EEG (iEEG) studies of free recall contrast words that are later recalled with words 
that are forgotten (the subsequent memory effect, SME), or study the retrieval period. 
As both conditions contain a word, this contrast measures how responses differ with later memory, 
but cancels out the response to the word itself. Only a few studies have examined the word-onset response directly. \citet{Kucewicz_2019} showed that high-gamma (HG) activity after word onset moves from visual cortex to the medial temporal lobe (MTL) and then to prefrontal cortex, and \citet{Marks_2021} found that theta, alpha/beta and gamma activity each spread across regions on their own timelines during word encoding.

\subsection*{High gamma and alpha as markers of cortical engagement }\label{intro-hg-alpha}
%(cut/reduce?)
Broadband HG (70--150 Hz) is widely used as a measure of local population spiking \citep{Manning_2009,Ray_2011}. 
At stimulus onset, alpha and beta power decrease \citep[event-related desynchronization;][]{Pfurtscheller_1999}; 
together, an alpha decrease and an HG increase mark a region engaged by a stimulus. 
Conversely, alpha increases in task-irrelevant regions are thought to mean suppression \citep{Jensen_2010}, 
so the same two measures can separate regions that a stimulus engages from regions it suppresses.

\subsection*{Timing of visual word processing}\label{intro-timing}
%(most imp.)
Reading a word progresses in stages: visual features are extracted within about 100 ms \citep{Thorpe_1996}, 
the letter string is recognised as a word at about 200 ms \citep{Cohen_2000}, its sound is activated 
automatically \citep{van_Orden_1987}, and its meaning is integrated with context by about 400 ms 
\citep{Kutas_1980}. These stages imply a reading network that runs from occipital cortex through left 
fusiform gyrus, which contains the visual word form area \citep[VWFA;][]{Cohen_2000,Dehaene_2011}, to 
temporal, supramarginal and inferior frontal cortex \citep{Price_2012,Taylor_2013}, with posterior 
responses to letter strings preceding more anterior responses to lexical status \citep{Nobre_1994,Allison_1994}. 
Much of this picture comes from scalp EEG, MEG, and fMRI, which resolve the order in which these stages 
occur but not precisely when and where, while iEEG records local activity with both 
millisecond and millimetre precision \citep{Parvizi_2018}. However, most intracranial studies of reading 
record from tens of patients with sparse, clinically determined coverage and focus on the ventral stream, 
and so the order in which regions across the whole brain respond to a word has not been measured within one 
large dataset.

\subsection*{Synchrony, expectation and memory}\label{intro-sync-expect-memory}

Beyond local activity, word onset may change how regions synchronize. Alpha phase synchrony is thought 
to coordinate distant regions \citep{Palva_2007}, and in free recall, low-frequency synchrony between regions 
increases during successful encoding and retrieval \citep{Solomon_2017,Rao_2025}. The response may also 
depend on when the word is expected: the blank before each word varies in length, and the longer it lasts, 
the more expected the word becomes \citep{Nobre_2017}. Finally, spectral features recorded while a word 
is studied predict whether it will be recalled \citep{Ezzyat_2017}, and comparing recalled with forgotten 
words region by region, HFA predicting later recall appears first in visual, fusiform and medial 
temporal regions and later in left inferior frontal, posterior parietal and ventrolateral temporal 
cortex \citep{Burke_2014a}. These approaches either pool features across the brain and the whole word 
period, or test each region and time point for a difference in mean activity. How well each region's 
single-event response predicts recall, and when that prediction arises, has not been measured with 
classifiers.

\subsection*{The present study }\label{intro-present}
%(change/delete heading)
Here we characterize the word-onset response in 362 neurosurgical patients performing delayed free recall, 
contrasting each word with the blank screen before it. We ask (1) which regions respond, and in what order;
(2) how regions synchronize, using phase and envelope connectivity; (3) whether the response depends 
on how long the subject waited for the word; (4) whether, where and when single-event responses
predict later recall; and (5) whether each subject's response is reliable across sessions.

\section*{Methods}\label{methods}

\subsection*{Data and cohort}\label{data-and-cohort}
We analyzed intracranial EEG (iEEG) recordings from 362 patients with pharmacoresistant epilepsy (x males, y females) who completed a delayed free-recall task while implanted with electrodes for clinical seizure monitoring. On each trial, subjects studied a list of words, completed an arithmetic distractor task (20--25 s of problems of the form ``$A + B + C = ??$'', with $A$, $B$ and $C$ drawn from the integers 1--9), and then recalled aloud as many words as they could during a 30 s retrieval period (45 s in a variant of the task). Lists were drawn from a pool of common English nouns; in catFR1, each list was built from words in a small number of semantic categories. A countdown preceded each list. Each word stayed on screen for 1600 ms and was followed by a blank screen of 750--1000 ms (800--1200 ms in the variant). A session consisted of 12 trials of 12 words (25 trials of 15 words in the variant). Data were collected as part of a multi-site collaboration at Columbia University Medical Center (New York, NY), Dartmouth-Hitchcock Medical Center (Lebanon, NH), Emory University Hospital (Atlanta, GA), the Hospital of the University of Pennsylvania (Philadelphia, PA), the Mayo Clinic (Rochester, MN), the National Institutes of Health (Bethesda, MD), the University of Colorado Anschutz Medical Campus (Aurora, CO), the University of Texas Health Science Center at San Antonio (San Antonio, TX), the University of Texas Southwestern Medical Center (Dallas, TX), and Thomas Jefferson University Hospital (Philadelphia, PA). Electrode type and placement were determined solely by clinical needs. Each site's institutional review board approved the protocol, and all patients gave informed consent. EEG was recorded at site-specific sampling rates (200--2000 Hz) and aligned to behavioral events using hardware synchronization pulses or online timing messages.

\subsection*{Events and analysis windows}\label{events}
We compared a ``word on'' window (0--600 ms after word onset) with a ``word off'' window ($-700$ to $-100$ ms, the blank screen before the same word), so that each pair of windows came from the same word presentation. The word-off window ends 100 ms before onset, and because the shortest blank is 750 ms, it starts at least 50 ms after the previous word left the screen. Both windows are 600 ms long, so spectral estimates have the same resolution and bias in each. We excluded the first word of each list, as it follows the countdown rather than a blank screen. After exclusions, X $\pm$ Y (mean $\pm$ SD across subjects) word events per session remained.

\subsection*{Preprocessing}\label{preprocessing}
Recordings were re-referenced to a bipolar montage by subtracting the signals of adjacent contacts \citep{Mercier_2022,Li_2018}. Word-on and word-off clips were loaded separately for each word, each extended by 500 ms of real (not mirrored) data on both sides to absorb filter edge artifacts. Clips were resampled to 500 Hz and filtered for line noise with 4th-order Butterworth band-stop filters ($\pm$2 Hz) at the mains frequency (60 Hz). To give every subject the same passband within the high-gamma range, we also notched the 100, 120 and 150 Hz harmonics in all recordings. The buffers were then removed before any further analysis.

We excluded bipolar pairs whose contacts were more than 20 mm apart and channels with flat signals. We excluded whole sessions with a missing or sub-500 Hz sampling rate, because after resampling to 500 Hz the 250 Hz Nyquist limit must sit well above the 70--150 Hz high-gamma band. We also excluded sessions with residual line noise, defined as a spectral peak at a non-notched mains harmonic more than five times the power at neighbouring frequencies (9 sessions). 

\subsection*{Electrode localization}\label{electrode-localization}
Electrode contacts were localized with the lab's standard pipeline. Each patient's pre-implant T1-weighted MRI was segmented and parcellated with FreeSurfer \citep{Fischl_2002,Fischl_2004,Desikan_2006}, and the post-implant CT was co-registered to it using Advanced Normalization Tools \citep{Avants_2008}. Contacts were identified manually and assigned anatomical labels automatically \citep{Dykstra_2012}; a subset of labels was reviewed by neuroradiologists. Where a T2-weighted MRI was available, medial temporal lobe subregions were labelled automatically \citep{Yushkevich_2014}. Each bipolar pair took the label at the midpoint of its two contacts, from volumetric labels for depth electrodes and surface labels for grid and strip electrodes \citep{Azarion_2014}. For pairs whose label was missing from the standard database, we recovered it from the imaging pipeline's own label files: either the pair's own midpoint label or, when the two contacts skipped a contact, that intermediate contact's label. We grouped labels into 12 regions of interest: frontal, temporal, parietal, occipital and limbic cortex (excluding the hippocampus), plus the hippocampus, each split by hemisphere \citep{Burke_2013}. Supplementary Table (ADD) lists the mapping from anatomical labels to regions.

\subsection*{Power computation}\label{power}
We estimated alpha (8--13 Hz) power, and the power spectrum from 5--150 Hz, with multitaper spectral estimation \citep{Thomson_1982,Slepian_1978,Percival_1993,Prerau_2017}, using a 2 Hz bandwidth over each 600 ms window. For the spectrum, power was averaged into 11 log-spaced frequency bins, leaving out $\pm$2 Hz around 50, 60, 100 and 120 Hz. We estimated high-gamma (70--150 Hz) power by band-pass filtering each clip into eight 10 Hz sub-bands and taking the squared magnitude of the Hilbert transform in each. Each sub-band was divided by its mean over all word-off events for that electrode, and the normalized sub-bands were then averaged. Every frequency in the band therefore carries equal weight, which suits high-frequency activity as a broadband signal rather than an oscillation \citep{Burke_2015a,Edwards_2009,Bruns_2004,Manning_2009,Ray_2011}. For each electrode and band, we summarized the word-on versus word-off change as Cohen's $d$ of per-event power, averaged over each window \citep{Cohen_2013,Lakens_2013}.

\subsection*{High-gamma latency}\label{high-gamma-latency}
We measured latency only at electrodes with a reliable high-gamma response, defined as per-event word-on power greater than word-off power (one-tailed Welch's $t$ test, Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction within each session; \citealp{WELCH_1947,Benjamini_1995}). This criterion selected 4950 of 122,339 electrode-sessions from 299 subjects. Because electrodes are selected and measured in the same data \citep{Kriegeskorte_2009}, the selection uses mean power, whereas latency comes from the response's normalized time course, so selection does not determine latency. For each electrode, we subtracted the word-off mean from the high-gamma envelope averaged over word events and smoothed it with a 30 ms boxcar. Latency was defined as the first time the curve reached half its peak within 0--800 ms (half-maximum latency; \citealp{Kiesel_2007,Liesefeld_2018}). Electrodes whose peak was not positive were excluded. Half-maximum latency is less affected than onset latency by the temporal smearing that filtering introduces \citep{VanRullen_2011}. We took each subject's median latency per region and report the mean $\pm$ SEM across subjects for the 12 regions and their anatomical subregions, pooling hemispheres.

\subsection*{Synchrony}\label{synchrony}
We measured alpha connectivity with the corrected imaginary phase-locking value (ciPLV), using multitaper spectral estimation with a 2 Hz bandwidth in MNE-Connectivity \citep{Gramfort_2013}. The ciPLV ignores zero-lag coupling and is therefore insensitive to volume conduction \citep{Nolte_2004,Bruna_2018}. We measured high-gamma connectivity with the corrected amplitude envelope correlation (AEC-c; \citealp{Hipp_2012}, also insensitive to volume conduction): signals were band-passed at 70--110 Hz, their Hilbert envelopes were pairwise orthogonalized and correlated, and the correlations were averaged over events. Limiting the band to 70--110 Hz keeps the envelope's spectrum below the carrier frequencies, so the Hilbert envelope is well defined \citep{Bedrosian_1963}. Pairs of bipolar channels that shared a contact were excluded \citep{Mercier_2017}. For each electrode, we averaged the word-on minus word-off change in synchrony over all its partners, giving that electrode's node strength / synchrony \citep{Rao_2025}. We treat synchrony as a secondary analysis, as an evoked response common to several regions can raise phase and envelope coupling without any interaction between them (see Discussion).

\subsubsection*{Time-resolved network}\label{time-resolved-network}
To follow how the alpha network develops over time, we computed synchrony in nine overlapping 200 ms epochs starting every 100 ms from 0--1000 ms after word onset. This range ends well before the response to the word's offset at 1600 ms. From each epoch we subtracted the mean of three 200 ms word-off epochs. We analyzed only region-connections recorded in at least 100 subjects. Because word onset changes synchrony across the whole network, a region's mean change over its connections would differ from zero merely by sharing in that change. We therefore defined a hub as a region that stands out from the network \citep{Sporns_2014,Rubinov_2010}: for each subject and epoch, its hub score is its mean change in synchrony over all of its connections minus the mean change over all connections that do not involve it. Hub scores were tested against zero across subjects with a $t$ test, Benjamini-Hochberg corrected over regions $\times$ epochs. For display, we plotted each significant hub's five strongest connections (by absolute mean change, of either sign), with line width proportional to $|t|$ \citep{Solomon_2017, Rao_2025}.

\subsection*{Mixed-effects models}\label{mixed-effects-models}
To compare latencies between lobes while accounting for multiple electrodes per subject, we fit a linear mixed-effects model in R with lme4 and tested contrasts with emmeans \citep{Bates_2015,Lenth_2025}. Latency, with one row per responsive electrode, was modeled as
\begin{align*}
\text{latency} \sim{} & \text{lobe} + (1\,|\,\text{sub}) + (1\,|\,\text{sub}{:}\text{lobe})\\
& + (1\,|\,\text{sub}{:}\text{region}),
\end{align*}
and all 15 pairwise lobe contrasts were Holm-corrected.

\subsection*{Anticipation}\label{anticipation}
To test whether the response depends on how long subjects waited for the word, we used the same responsive electrodes as in the latency analysis and split each session's word events at its median blank length. For each electrode, we expressed the high-gamma response, averaged over word events, after long and after short blanks relative to the same baseline, the electrode's word-off mean over all events. A difference between groups therefore cannot come from a difference in their baselines. The effect was the peak response (0--800 ms) after long blanks minus the peak after short blanks. We averaged effects per subject and region and tested them against zero with one-sample $t$ tests, Benjamini-Hochberg corrected across regions.

\subsection*{Recall prediction}\label{recall-prediction}
To test when and where the word-onset response predicts later recall (encoding contrast: recalled vs.\ not recalled words), we averaged the high-gamma response (computed as for latency) in 100 ms windows from 0--1000 ms and z-scored it within each session. For every subject, lobe (at least 5 electrodes) and window, we trained an L2-regularized logistic regression with the penalty used by \citet[$C = 7.2\times10^{-4}$;][]{Ezzyat_2017} and balanced class weights. Models were cross-validated by leaving out one session at a time, or one list at a time for subjects with a single session. Performance was the area under the ROC curve (AUC) of the pooled held-out predictions. Across subjects, we tested AUC $> 0.5$ with one-sided $t$ tests, Benjamini-Hochberg corrected over lobes $\times$ windows. We also computed chance AUC by permutation (about 0.493); it is computed as a reference but was not used in the tests.

\subsection*{Reliability}\label{reliability}
To estimate test-retest reliability, we randomly divided each subject's sessions into two halves of at least two sessions each. Within each half, we averaged Cohen's $d$ (power) or hub strength (each electrode's mean change in synchrony over all its connections, as in Fig.~5A) per anatomical region. Reliability was the Pearson correlation between the two halves across that subject's regions. To ask which lobes' network results replicate across patients (replicability, as opposed to the within-subject reliability above), we pooled hemispheres and took each lobe's profile: its change in synchrony on each connection to the six lobes (itself included) in each of the nine epochs of the time-resolved network. We split the subjects 1000 times into two disjoint random halves of 127 subjects each, the same size for every lobe, and correlated the two halves' mean profiles (Pearson $r$).

\subsection*{Statistics}\label{statistics}
Unless stated otherwise, the subject was the unit of analysis. Values were averaged over sessions for each electrode and then over electrodes for each subject and region. Each region was tested against zero with a one-sample $t$ test across subjects, analyzing only regions recorded in at least 30 subjects. Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction was applied within each figure and band \citep{Holmes_1998,Benjamini_1995,Seabold_2010}. For the spectral profiles (Figure~\ref{fig:power}B, Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:spectrum-fine}), as in \citet{DeHaan_2025}, every region (or subregion) $\times$ frequency cell was tested in the same way and corrected over all cells of the map.


\section*{Results}\label{results}

\subsection*{1. Power changes at word
onset}\label{power-changes-at-word-onset}

Word onset decreased alpha power and increased high-gamma power most strongly in occipital cortex (Figure~\ref{fig:power}A; alpha d = $-0.13$ left, $-0.10$ right; high gamma d = $+0.40$ left, $+0.29$ right). Alpha also decreased slightly in hippocampus, right limbic and right temporal cortex (d $-0.016$ to $-0.025$), and increased slightly in frontal (d $+0.023$ to $+0.031$) and left parietal cortex ($+0.024$). High gamma increased slightly in limbic cortex, hippocampus, and left temporal and parietal cortex (d $+0.02$ to $+0.05$), and did not change in frontal cortex.

Across the spectrum (Figure~\ref{fig:power}B), 70 of 132 region $\times$ frequency cells changed (FDR $q < .05$). Occipital cortex showed an alpha--beta decrease ($t = -7.6$ at 11 Hz, left) and a broadband increase above 37 Hz ($t$ up to 8.7). Frontal cortex instead showed a theta/low-alpha increase ($t$ up to 11.7 at 6 Hz) and a beta decrease ($t$ down to $-13.4$ at 27 Hz). Limbic cortex and hippocampus showed a weaker version of the occipital pattern (alpha down, high gamma up to $t = 6.1$). Left temporal cortex increased at high gamma ($t$ up to 4.4), whereas right temporal cortex decreased at 8--20 Hz.

Per anatomical subregion (Supplementary Figure~\ref{fig:spectrum-fine}; 153 of 341 cells), the occipital pattern was clearest in fusiform gyrus ($t = -7.8$ at 11 Hz, $+8.7$ at 69 Hz) and lateral occipital cortex ($+7.5$ at 69 Hz), and also appeared in parahippocampal gyrus ($-6.7$ at 11 Hz, $+6.5$ at 51 Hz), superior parietal lobule ($+4.5$ at 69 Hz) and inferior temporal gyrus ($+4.2$ at 69 Hz). The frontal pattern was strongest in middle frontal gyrus ($+11.7$ at 6 Hz, $-13.2$ at 27 Hz), inferior frontal gyrus and orbitofrontal cortex, and also appeared in insula. Responses differed within regions: superior temporal gyrus decreased from 6 to 37 Hz ($t$ down to $-6.9$), whereas inferior temporal gyrus increased above 37 Hz and middle temporal gyrus barely changed ($|t| \leq 2.9$).

\subsection*{2. Order of activation}\label{order-of-activation}

\begin{figure*}[!tp]
\centering
\panelc{A}{0.6}{img/fig4b_latency_fine.png}\hfill\panelc{B}{0.38}{img/fig4c_latency_mixed.png}
\caption{Order of high-gamma responses after word onset. (A) Half-max latency per anatomical subregion (left; mean $\pm$ SEM across subjects; n: subjects) and the average high-gamma response over time, as a fraction of its peak (right; shading: 95\% CI across subjects). (B) Estimated latency per lobe from a mixed-effects model (mean $\pm$ SE); brackets: neighbouring lobes in the latency order (Holm over all 15 pairs).}
\label{fig:latency}
\end{figure*}

High-gamma responses began in occipital cortex and moved forward (Figure~\ref{fig:latency}A). Half-maximum latency was 132 ms in right and 156 ms in left occipital cortex, about 165 ms in limbic cortex, 171--177 ms in right temporal and right parietal cortex, 201--210 ms in hippocampus and left temporal cortex, 222--227 ms in right frontal and left parietal cortex, and 245 ms in left frontal cortex. Subregions recorded in at least 30 subjects followed the same order: lateral occipital cortex (123 ms), fusiform and lingual gyri (156--158 ms), parahippocampal (168 ms) and superior parietal cortex (175 ms), temporal regions and hippocampus (183--209 ms), middle frontal, supramarginal, precentral and inferior frontal cortex (213--238 ms), and orbitofrontal cortex (316 ms). Early regions peaked sharply at about 150 ms, whereas hippocampus, insula and frontal cortex peaked broadly at about 350--550 ms.

The mixed-effects model gave the same order across lobes (Figure~\ref{fig:latency}B): occipital 150 ms, limbic 170, temporal 193, hippocampus 209, parietal 210 and frontal 238 ms. Nine of the 15 lobe pairs differed (Holm $p < .05$). For example, occipital cortex responded before temporal, parietal, hippocampal and frontal cortex, and frontal cortex responded after every lobe except hippocampus ($p = .10$; 67 subjects). Of the neighbouring lobes in this order, only parietal and frontal cortex differed ($p = .024$). The posterior-to-anterior order therefore holds across two or more steps, but not between every pair of neighbours.

\subsection*{3. Synchrony}\label{synchrony-1}

\begin{figure*}[!tp]
\centering
\panel{A}{0.55}{img/fig5_synchrony.png}\\[6pt]
\panel{B}{1}{img/fig6c_epochs_alpha.png}
\caption{Synchrony at word onset. (A) Change in alpha ciPLV (top) and high-gamma AEC-c (bottom) per region, word on vs.\ off: each electrode's mean change over all partners, averaged per subject; mean $\pm$ SEM across subjects; stars: BH-FDR. Red (blue) nodes: hubs, regions whose mean change over their connections is larger (smaller) than that of the rest of the network (BH-FDR over regions $\times$ windows); lines: each hub's five strongest connections (red: synchronized, blue: desynchronized; width $\propto |t|$).}
\label{fig:synchrony}
\end{figure*}

Alpha ciPLV increased at word onset in 9 of 12 regions, all except left and right hippocampus and right limbic cortex (Figure~\ref{fig:synchrony}A). The increase was about twice as large in occipital cortex ($\approx$0.0015) as elsewhere ($\approx$0.0003--0.0009). High-gamma AEC-c did not change significantly in any region.

Over time (Figure~\ref{fig:synchrony}B), alpha synchrony across the whole network rose in every window (mean change over all connections, $t$ = 3.3--7.5). Against this background, occipital cortex stood out as a hub only in the earliest windows (0--300 ms; right occipital to 400 ms). Left hippocampus was less synchronized than the rest of the network at 100--600 and 700--900 ms, as were left temporal cortex at 0--300 ms and right temporal cortex at 100--300 ms; among subregions, left hippocampus was below the network at 200--400 ms. High gamma had no region hubs; among subregions, only right inferior frontal gyrus stood above the network (100--300 ms; not shown).

word)}\label{anticipation-blank-length-before-the-word}

\begin{figure*}[!tp]
\centering
\panelc{A}{0.36}{img/fig9a_wait_times.png}\hfill\panelc{B}{0.62}{img/fig9_anticipation.png}
\caption{Temporal expectation. The occipital high-gamma peak was larger after long than after short waits (Figure~\ref{fig:anticipation}B; left $+0.072$, $q = .022$; right $+0.049$, $q = .029$), and no other region differed. Both groups were measured against the same baseline, so this is not a baseline difference. It is consistent with temporal expectation enhancing visual responses \citep[][monkey V1]{Lima_2011,Nobre_2017}, although after short waits the response may also have recovered less from the previous word.

\subsection*{5. Recall prediction over the response (encoding
contrast)}\label{recall-prediction-over-the-response-encoding-contrast}

\begin{figure*}[!tp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{img/fig7_recall_time.png}
\caption{Recall prediction from the high-gamma response (encoding contrast: recalled vs.\ not recalled). AUC per lobe and 100 ms window, mean $\pm$ SEM across subjects; dashed line: 0.5; stars: AUC $>0.5$, BH-FDR over lobe $\times$ window.}
\label{fig:recall}

No lobe predicted recall before 200 ms (Figure~\ref{fig:recall}). Occipital cortex predicted recall first, at 200--700 ms (peak AUC 0.519 at 500--600 ms), and frontal cortex predicted it from 300 ms through 1000 ms (peak 0.525 at 800--900 ms). Temporal cortex predicted recall at 400--900 ms, parietal cortex at 300--500 and 600--800 ms, hippocampus at 500--600 ms and limbic cortex at 600--700 ms. AUCs were low ($\leq$ 0.525) because each classifier used one lobe's high gamma in one 100 ms window. This early-posterior, late-frontal order matches the subsequent memory effect in high-frequency activity \citep{Burke_2014a}.

\subsection*{6. Reliability across
sessions}\label{reliability-across-sessions}

\begin{figure*}[!tp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{img/fig8_reliability.png}
\caption{Session-to-session reliability: per subject, Pearson r across regions between two random halves of the subject's sessions, for power (left) and synchrony hub strength (middle); dashed lines: medians. (Right) Replicability across subjects of each lobe's connectivity profile: its change on each connection $\times$ window, hemispheres pooled, correlated between two disjoint halves of the subjects (127 each); box plots over 1000 splits (whiskers: 1.5 IQR; outliers not shown).}
\label{fig:reliability}
\end{figure*}

Power maps were reliable across sessions (Figure~\ref{fig:reliability}, left; 85 subjects with at least two sessions per half): median $r$ = 0.67 for alpha and 0.81 for high gamma. Synchrony maps were much less reliable (middle; median $r$ = 0.19 for alpha ciPLV, 0.14 for high-gamma AEC-c).

Across subjects (right; 127 subjects per half), occipital cortex's connectivity profile replicated well (median $r$ = 0.90 for alpha, 0.80 for high gamma), while every other lobe replicated weakly (alpha $\approx$0.3--0.4, high gamma $\approx$0.1--0.2). Occipital replicability stayed at 0.89 after removing each window's mean, so it reflects the pattern over connections, not only a shared time course. In the other lobes, replicability came largely from their connection with occipital cortex. Without it, alpha $r$ fell from 0.43 to 0.17 in limbic cortex and from 0.32 to 0.03 in hippocampus, and remained weak in frontal (0.28), parietal (0.35) and temporal cortex (0.22).

\section*{Discussion}\label{discussion}

The occipital alpha decrease and HG increase match the activation response first described in sensorimotor cortex \citep{Crone_1998a,Crone_1998b} and in visual cortex \citep{Lachaux_2005}, including for pseudowords and consonant strings, where the electrodes selective in each band overlapped little \citep{Vidal_2010}. The same low-frequency decrease and HFA increase marks successful encoding \citep{Sederberg_2003,Long_2014}, and this broadband tilt also appears outside memory, during arithmetic \citep{Randazzo_2019}, where it predicts response speed, mainly in frontal regions \citep{DeHaan_2025}. The frontal low-frequency increase and beta decrease, without a frontal HG increase, fit active suppression of cortex not needed for the task \citep{Jensen_2010}; HG similarly decreases during tasks in default-mode regions \citep{Ossand_n_2011}.

Our latencies agree with smaller iEEG studies. Gamma activity begins at about 150 ms in basal temporal cortex, reaches the VWFA at about 200 ms, and reaches superior temporal and Broca's areas after 400 ms \citep{Mainy_2007}; word-selective ventral temporal electrodes respond at about 150 ms \citep{Kadipasaoglu_2016}; and the effects of word properties move anteriorly along the ventral stream, with feedback to posterior regions at 250--500 ms \citep{Woolnough_2020}. Beyond the ventral stream, MEG shows responses proceeding from temporal cortex to Broca's area and finally to orbitofrontal cortex at about 370 ms \citep{Halgren_2002}, the same order we find. The sharp early and broad late responses we observe are consistent with feedforward input followed by feedback \citep{Halgren_2006,Ghuman_2014}.

While we observe a substantial decrease in alpha power in the occipital cortex, its alpha synchrony with other regions rises, consistent with \citep{Hanslmayr_2016}. Synchrony measured at stimulus onset is, however, harder to interpret than power: an evoked response shared across regions can raise phase and envelope coupling without any interaction between them, and cannot be cleanly separated from cognitive effects \citealp{David_2006,Shah_2004}. Subtracting the average evoked response does not fully remove it, because its amplitude and latency vary across word presentations \citep{Truccolo_2002,David_2006}. The absence of HG envelope coupling is consistent with HFA being local and asynchronous; studies of memory show that regions with gamma increases during successful encoding lose global synchrony \citep{Burke_2013}.

\subsection*{Expectation}\label{disc-expectation}

Temporally expected events evoke larger visual responses: in monkey V1, gamma increases and alpha decreases when a stimulus is expected \citep{Lima_2011}, and expectation speeds and enhances visual processing in humans \citep{Doherty_2005}. The larger occipital response after longer waits fits this account. Because long- and short-wait events were measured against the same baseline, averaged over all events, the effect cannot come from a raised baseline after short waits; an alternative is that the visual response recovers less from the previous word when the interval is short.

\subsection*{Recall prediction}\label{disc-recall}

Recall prediction appeared only after the early visual response, briefly in occipital cortex and persistently in frontal cortex, in the same direction as the early posterior and late frontal HFA effects found by comparing recalled with forgotten words \citep{Burke_2014a}. These classifiers may partly track general fluctuations in brain state rather than encoding of the item \citep{Weidemann_2021}.
