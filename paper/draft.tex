\subsection{Introduction}\label{introduction}

\subsubsection{1. What is known about the word-onset response in this
paradigm}\label{what-is-known-about-the-word-onset-response-in-this-paradigm}

Most intracranial EEG (iEEG) studies of free recall contrast words that
are later recalled with words that are forgotten (the subsequent memory
effect, SME), or study the retrieval period (Sederberg et al.~2003,
2007; Burke et al.~2013; Long et al.~2014; Solomon et al.~2017;
Rubinstein et al.~2023). Because both conditions contain a word, this
contrast measures how responses differ with later memory, but cancels
out the response to the word itself. Only a few studies have examined
the word-onset response directly. Long et al.~(2020, preprint)
identified electrodes with a high-gamma (HG) response to words in 151
patients performing the same task and used representational similarity
analysis to time when word features are represented. Burke, Long et
al.~(2014) found that the high-frequency activity (HFA) SME appears
first in posterior regions and later in anterior ones. Kucewicz et
al.~(2019) showed that HG after word onset moves from visual cortex to
the medial temporal lobe (MTL) and then to prefrontal cortex, and Marks
et al.~(2021) found that theta, alpha/beta and gamma activity each
spread across regions on their own timelines during word encoding.

\subsubsection{2. High gamma as local activation, alpha/beta decrease as
release from
inhibition}\label{high-gamma-as-local-activation-alphabeta-decrease-as-release-from-inhibition}

Broadband HG (70-150 Hz) is widely used as a measure of local population
spiking (Manning et al.~2009; Ray \& Maunsell 2011; Lachaux et
al.~2012), although it may also reflect synaptic input (Leszczyński et
al.~2020), and is better described as a broadband signal than as an
oscillation (Burke, Ramayya \& Kahana 2015). At stimulus onset, alpha
and beta power decrease, a phenomenon known as event-related
desynchronization (Pfurtscheller \& Aranibar 1977; Pfurtscheller \&
Lopes da Silva 1999; Klimesch 1999, 2012; Klimesch et al.~2007). This
decrease is thought to release cortex from alpha-mediated inhibition
(Jensen \& Mazaheri 2010; Haegens et al.~2011). An alpha/beta decrease
together with a gamma increase is the typical cortical activation
response, first described in the sensorimotor cortex with
electrocorticography (Crone et al.~1998a, b) and observed in the visual
cortex in iEEG (Lachaux et al.~2005; Tallon-Baudry et al.~2005). Vidal
et al.~(2010) found this pattern for pseudowords and consonant strings,
with less than 10\% spatial overlap between the electrodes selective in
each band. Visual stimuli can evoke both narrowband gamma oscillations
and broadband gamma (Hermes et al.~2015; Bartoli et al.~2019). Occipital
alpha is strong and reactive to visual input (Geller et al.~2014;
Frauscher et al.~2018), and propagates from higher-order to lower-order
visual cortex (Halgren M et al.~2019). Gamma activity in
occipitotemporal cortex also carries information about the identity of
individual letters and words (Jacobs \& Kahana 2009). Not every region
is engaged by a stimulus. Alpha increases in task-irrelevant cortex are
thought to actively inhibit it (Jensen \& Mazaheri 2010), and HG often
decreases during tasks, particularly in default-mode regions (Ossandón
et al.~2011; Kucyi et al.~2020; Das, de los Angeles \& Menon 2022).
Frontal low-frequency increases and beta decreases can, however, also
signal engagement: frontal theta increases accompany cognitive control
(Cavanagh \& Frank 2014; Raghavachari et al.~2001), and beta decreases
have been linked to a change in the current cognitive set (Engel \&
Fries 2010; Spitzer \& Haegens 2017).

\subsubsection{3. The spectral signature of encoding (alpha, gamma,
spectral
tilt)}\label{the-spectral-signature-of-encoding-alpha-gamma-spectral-tilt}

Successful encoding (encoding contrast: recalled vs not recalled) is
marked by a decrease in low-frequency power and an increase in HFA
(Sederberg et al.~2003, 2007; Burke et al.~2013; Long et al.~2014;
Greenberg et al.~2015; Kucewicz et al.~2017, 2018; Marks et al.~2021;
Katerman et al.~2022; Rubinstein et al.~2023). The
information-via-desynchronization hypothesis proposes that alpha/beta
power decreases allow neural populations to represent richer
information, so that larger decreases accompany deeper encoding
(Hanslmayr et al.~2009, 2011, 2012; Hanslmayr \& Staudigl 2014). Whether
these effects are band-specific or reflect a single broadband tilt of
the spectrum, with low frequencies falling and high frequencies rising,
remains debated (Podvalny et al.~2015; Burke et al.~2015; Rudoler et
al.~2023; Fellner et al.~2019; Broitman \& Kahana 2026, preprint); the
aperiodic background changes at stimulus onset (Gyurkovics et al.~2022)
and can be separated from oscillatory peaks (Donoghue et al.~2020). Tilt
also appears beyond memory, during arithmetic (Randazzo et al.~2019,
preprint), where it predicts slower responses, mainly in frontal regions
(DeHaan et al.~2025, preprint).

\subsubsection{4. Timing of visual word processing (latency
figure)}\label{timing-of-visual-word-processing-latency-figure}

Reading a word unfolds in stages: visual features are extracted within
about 100 ms (Thorpe et al.~1996; Schmolesky et al.~1998), the letter
string is recognised as a word at about 200 ms (Cohen et al.~2000), its
sound is activated automatically (Van Orden 1987), and its meaning is
integrated with context at about 400 ms (Kutas \& Hillyard 1980). Visual
words are processed along a ventral stream hierarchy, from posterior
responses to letter strings to more anterior responses sensitive to
lexical status (Nobre et al.~1994; Allison et al.~1994, 1999; Lerner et
al.~2001; Grainger \& Holcomb 2009; Carreiras et al.~2014; Grill-Spector
\& Weiner 2014). One account gives a central role to a visual word form
area (VWFA) in left fusiform cortex (Cohen et al.~2000; Dehaene \& Cohen
2011), while an interactive account emphasises top-down feedback to
ventral occipitotemporal cortex (Price \& Devlin 2011). iEEG studies
report HG latencies close to ours. Word-selective electrodes respond at
153 ms in left ventral temporal cortex and 194 ms in left lateral
occipital cortex (Kadipasaoglu et al.~2016), compared with 157 ms in
fusiform here. Gamma activity begins at 150 ms in basal temporal cortex,
reaches the VWFA at 200 ms, and reaches superior temporal and Broca's
areas after 400 ms (Mainy et al.~2008). Word-length effects appear at
the occipital pole at 75 ms and move anteriorly, word-frequency effects
appear at 150 ms in mid-fusiform cortex, and word selectivity feeds back
to posterior regions at 250--500 ms (Woolnough et al.~2021).
Letter-selective responses precede word-selective ones by about 60 ms
(Thesen et al.~2012), ventral temporal signals begin at about 100 ms
(Jacques et al.~2016), and fusiform responses consist of a sharp early
peak followed by sustained activity (Ghuman et al.~2014).
Scene-selective parahippocampal cortex responds from about 80 ms (Bastin
et al.~2013). Further iEEG work has characterised reading responses in
left mid-fusiform and inferotemporal cortex (Gaillard et al.~2006;
Hamamé et al.~2013; Hirshorn et al.~2016; Lochy et al.~2018).

Beyond the ventral stream, MEG shows semantic responses proceeding from
Wernicke's area to anterior temporal cortex, Broca's area, and finally
orbital/frontopolar cortex at about 370 ms (Halgren et al.~2002), the
same order as ours with orbitofrontal cortex last. Activity converges in
anterior temporal and inferior prefrontal cortex at about 400 ms
(Marinkovic et al.~2003), and Broca's area shows lexical, grammatical
and phonological stages at about 200, 320 and 450 ms (Sahin et
al.~2009). Other work confirms that temporal cortex precedes frontal
cortex in reading and naming (Chan et al.~2011; Flinker et al.~2015;
Forseth et al.~2018; Woolnough et al.~2022, 2023). The contrast between
sharp early and broad late peaks may reflect feedforward input at
120--170 ms followed by feedback after about 550 ms, as seen in laminar
recordings (Halgren et al.~2006). Hippocampal responses come last in
face recognition, at about 480 ms (Barbeau et al.~2008), and anterior
medial temporal cortex shows a late N400 to words (McCarthy et
al.~1995). MTL single neurons respond in the same order as our regions
but later: parahippocampal at 271 ms, hippocampus at 394 ms and amygdala
at 397 ms (Mormann et al.~2008; see also Kreiman et al.~2000; Quiroga et
al.~2005).

\subsubsection{5. Synchrony: alpha phase coupling vs high-gamma envelope
coupling (methods and findings, may move to
Methods)}\label{synchrony-alpha-phase-coupling-vs-high-gamma-envelope-coupling-methods-and-findings-may-move-to-methods}

Alpha phase synchrony is thought to coordinate distant regions actively
rather than reflect idling (Palva \& Palva 2007, 2011; Doesburg et
al.~2009; Palva et al.~2010; Bonnefond et al.~2017), and occipital alpha
gates the flow of information to object-selective cortex (Zumer et
al.~2014). In visual cortex, alpha/beta rhythms carry feedback and gamma
carries feedforward signals (van Kerkoerle et al.~2014; Bastos et
al.~2015; Michalareas et al.~2016; Mejias et al.~2016), consistent with
communication through coherence (Fries 2005, 2015); this offers one
reading of the occipital alpha hubs at 0--400 ms. Alpha power can fall
while alpha synchrony between regions rises (Hanslmayr et al.~2016),
matching our combination of decreased alpha power with increased ciPLV.
Phase synchrony supports memory in iEEG (Fell et al.~2001; Watrous et
al.~2013; Fell \& Axmacher 2011; Solomon et al.~2017, 2019), including
theta hubs during retrieval in the same dataset (Rao et al.~2025), and
long-range synchrony during word encoding predicts memory in scalp EEG
(Weiss \& Rappelsberger 2000). Conscious visual perception is likewise
accompanied by long-range synchrony (Rodriguez et al.~1999; Fisch et
al.~2009; Gaillard et al.~2009). In contrast, HFA appears to be local
and asynchronous: regions with gamma increases during successful
encoding (encoding contrast) lose global synchrony (Burke et al.~2013),
HFA desynchronizes during successful encoding and retrieval (Solomon et
al.~2017), and once evoked activity is removed, tasks tend to reduce
correlations between areas (Ito et al.~2020). These findings are
consistent with our null HG AEC-c result. Other studies, however, report
HG envelope coupling that depends on the task (Vidal et al.~2012; Foster
et al.~2015) and widespread co-bursting of high-frequency oscillations
during word encoding (Prathapagiri et al.~2026). Directed connectivity
has also been studied in these data (Das \& Menon 2021--2024).

Metric choice matters for these comparisons. ciPLV removes zero-lag
coupling caused by volume conduction (Lachaux et al.~1999; Nolte et
al.~2004; Bruña et al.~2018), as do related phase metrics (Vinck et
al.~2010, 2011), and orthogonalized amplitude envelope correlation
removes zero-lag leakage from envelope coupling (Bruns et al.~2000;
Brookes et al.~2012; Hipp et al.~2012; Colclough et al.~2015). Phase and
amplitude coupling are distinct modes of interaction (Siegel et
al.~2012; Engel et al.~2013; Siems \& Siegel 2020). Leakage-robust
metrics can still produce spurious ``ghost'' edges next to true ones
(Haufe et al.~2013; Bastos \& Schoffelen 2016; Palva et al.~2018), which
is relevant to the strongest-edge networks. Orthogonalized envelope
correlation is more reliable across sessions than lag-based phase
metrics (Colclough et al.~2016). Finally, connectivity in iEEG depends
strongly on distance between contacts (Mercier et al.~2017; Betzel et
al.~2019).

\subsubsection{7. Anticipation (jittered blank before the
word)}\label{anticipation-jittered-blank-before-the-word}

Reaction times and neural responses depend on how long the participant
waits for a stimulus and on the changing probability that it will appear
(the hazard rate; Niemi \& Näätänen 1981; Coull \& Nobre 1998, 2008;
Nobre et al.~2007; Nobre \& van Ede 2018), and single neurons encode the
hazard rate (Ghose \& Maunsell 2002; Janssen \& Shadlen 2005).
Temporally expected events evoke larger visual responses: in monkey V1,
gamma increases and alpha decreases when a stimulus is expected (Lima et
al.~2011), and expectation speeds and enhances visual processing in
humans (Doherty et al.~2005; Vangkilde et al.~2012). Human recordings
show that cortical rhythms entrain to the expected timing of events
(Lakatos et al.~2008; Stefanics et al.~2010; Besle et al.~2011).
Pre-stimulus alpha follows temporal expectation (Rohenkohl \& Nobre
2011; Cravo et al.~2011, 2013) and biases perception (Thut et al.~2006;
van Dijk et al.~2008; Busch et al.~2009; Mathewson et al.~2009; Iemi et
al.~2017; Samaha et al.~2020). We found no iEEG study of how a longer
inter-stimulus interval (ISI) affects responses during verbal encoding.

\subsubsection{8. Does the word-onset response predict
recall?}\label{does-the-word-onset-response-predict-recall}

Spectral features recorded while a word is studied predict whether it
will be recalled (encoding contrast; L2-regularised logistic regression;
Ezzyat et al.~2017, 2018), although these classifiers partly track
general fluctuations in brain state rather than encoding of the item
(Weidemann \& Kahana 2021). In the same contrast, HFA predicting later
recall appears first in visual, fusiform and medial temporal regions
(about 0--500 ms) and later in left inferior frontal, posterior parietal
and ventrolateral temporal cortex (about 400--900 ms; Burke, Long et
al.~2014). Activity before a stimulus can also predict memory:
pre-stimulus hippocampal theta predicts later recognition but not recall
(Merkow et al.~2014), pre-stimulus MTL and cortical low-frequency
activity predict encoding success in iEEG (Fell et al.~2011; Haque et
al.~2015), and similar effects appear in fMRI, MEG and scalp EEG (Otten
et al.~2006; Guderian et al.~2009; Addante et al.~2011).

\subsubsection{9. Gap this paper fills}\label{gap-this-paper-fills}

To our knowledge, this is the first whole-brain map of the response to
word onset itself, contrasting each word with the blank screen before
it, rather than contrasting recalled with forgotten words. With 362
patients, it gives a complete set of HG onset latencies across all
regions, extending the existing literature. It also tests phase (alpha
ciPLV) and envelope (HG AEC-c) coupling in the same data. Other open
free-recall iEEG datasets could be used to replicate these results
(Cimbalnik et al.~2025).

\subsection{Methods}\label{methods}

\subsubsection{Data and cohort}\label{data-and-cohort}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  OpenNeuro BIDS releases of the RAM / Penn free-recall data (FR1,
  catFR1) and pyFR, bipolar montage. OpenNeuro ds004789 (FR1), ds004809
  (catFR1), ds004865 (pyFR).
\item
  362 subjects, 1009 sessions: FR1 232 subj / 466 sess, catFR1 212 /
  465, pyFR 38 / 78.
\item
  Bipolar channels whose contacts are \textgreater{} 20 mm apart, and
  flat channels are dropped.
\item
  Exclusions: missing / \textless{} 499 Hz sampling rate, denylist /
  manual, mains-harmonic peak \textgreater{} 5× its neighbours at a
  non-notched frequency (9 sessions, all 100 Hz peaks at 60 Hz sites).
\item
  Contrast: \textbf{word on} (0--600 ms after word onset) vs
  \textbf{word off} (−700 to −100 ms, the blank screen before the same
  word). Words on screen 1600 s; blank between words
  \textasciitilde750-1250 ms.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Preprocessing}\label{preprocessing}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Word-off and word-on clips loaded separately per word, each with 500
  ms of real data buffer on both sides.
\item
  Resampled to 500 Hz, notch 60 Hz + 120 Hz harmonic (4th-order
  Butterworth, ±2 Hz); buffer cropped before every estimate. (Widmann et
  al.~2015; de Cheveigné \& Nelken 2019; Gramfort et al.~2013; Virtanen
  et al.~2020)
\item
  Why 500 ms buffer: resampling (FFT) and notching each short clip ring
  at its edges for \textgreater{} 50 ms; the ringing is simultaneous
  across channels and biased the amplitude-correlation measure (AEC-c).
  No effect on multitaper alpha (Supplement)
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Electrode localization}\label{electrode-localization}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Bipolar pairs labelled by lab localization default (depth: volumetric;
  grid/strip: surface). (Desikan et al.~2006; Fischl et al.~2002, 2004;
  Avants et al.~2008; Wang et al.~2013; Yushkevich et al.~2015; Azarion
  et al.~2014 (check); bipolar: Mercier et al.~2022; Li et al.~2018)
\item
  12 Burke ROIs: frontal, temporal, parietal, occipital, limbic,
  hippocampus × hemisphere; sub regions = anatomical labels within them.
  (Burke et al.~2013)
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Power}\label{power}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Alpha (8--13 Hz) and 5--100 Hz spectrum: multitaper, 2 Hz bandwidth,
  600 ms windows; spectrum in 10 log-spaced bins (48--52 / 58--62 Hz
  excluded). (Thomson 1982; Slepian 1978; Percival \& Walden 1993;
  Prerau et al.~2017)
\item
  High gamma (70--150 Hz): Hilbert in 8 × 10 Hz sub-bands, each divided
  by its word-off mean, averaged (broadband, every frequency weighted
  equally). (Edwards et al.~2009 (check); Bruns 2004; Manning et
  al.~2009; Ray \& Maunsell 2011)
\item
  Per electrode: Cohen's d of per-trial power, word on vs word off.
  (Cohen 1988; Lakens 2013)
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{High-gamma latency}\label{high-gamma-latency}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Responsive electrodes: per-trial high-gamma power word on
  \textgreater{} word off (one-tailed Welch t test, BH-FDR within
  session): 5004 of 122,339 electrode-sessions, 293 subjects. (Welch
  1947; Benjamini \& Hochberg 1995; selection-then-measure: Kriegeskorte
  et al.~2009)
\item
  Latency: trial-mean envelope minus word-off mean, 30 ms smoothing,
  first time it reaches half its peak within 0--800 ms (half-maximum).
  (Kiesel et al.~2008; Liesefeld 2018; filter smearing: VanRullen 2011)
\item
  Subject median per region → mean ± SEM across subjects; 12 ROIs and
  subregions (hemispheres pooled).
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Synchrony}\label{synchrony}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Alpha: ciPLV (MNE \texttt{spectral\_connectivity\_epochs}, multitaper,
  2 Hz bandwidth). (Bruña et al.~2018; Nolte et al.~2004;
  MNE-Connectivity (Zenodo))
\item
  High gamma: AEC-c (70--110 Hz band-pass + Hilbert,
  \texttt{envelope\_correlation}, pairwise orthogonalized, signed, mean
  over trials); 70--110 Hz so the envelope stays below the band
  (Bedrosian). (Hipp et al.~2012; Bedrosian 1963)
\item
  Pairs sharing a contact excluded. (Mercier et al.~2017) Per electrode:
  mean on − off change over all partners (Rao et al.~2025).
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Time-resolved network
(epochs)}\label{time-resolved-network-epochs}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  9 overlapping 200 ms epochs every 100 ms, 0--1000 ms (stops before the
  word's offset response at 1600 ms), each minus the mean of three 200
  ms word-off epochs.
\item
  Region pairs with ≥ 100 subjects. Hub (Rao): a region's mean change
  over its connections; t across subjects; two-stage FDR
  (Benjamini--Krieger--Yekutieli) over region × epoch. (Benjamini et
  al.~2006; Rubinov \& Sporns 2010)
\item
  Edges: each significant hub's 5 strongest connections (\textbar mean
  change\textbar), either sign; width ∝ \textbar t\textbar{} (Solomon et
  al.~2017 style).
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{Mixed-effects models (R lme4 / emmeans;
\texttt{mixed\_models.R})}{Mixed-effects models (R lme4 / emmeans; mixed\_models.R)}}\label{mixed-effects-models-r-lme4-emmeans-mixed_models.r}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Latency: latency\_ms \textasciitilde{} lobe + (1\textbar sub) +
  (1\textbar sub:lobe) + (1\textbar sub:region), one row per responsive
  electrode; all 15 lobe pairs, Holm.
\item
  Power: cohens\_d \textasciitilde{} lobe × band + (1 +
  band\textbar sub) + (1\textbar sub:lobe) + (1\textbar sub:lobe:band) +
  (1\textbar sub:region) + (1\textbar sub:label); lobe × band
  interaction contrast per lobe pair, Holm.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Anticipation}\label{anticipation}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Responsive electrodes (as latency); trials split at the session's
  median blank length; peak (0--800 ms) of the trial-mean high-gamma
  response after long minus short waits; subject mean per ROI,
  one-sample t, BH-FDR over ROIs.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Recall prediction (encoding contrast: recalled vs not
recalled)}\label{recall-prediction-encoding-contrast-recalled-vs-not-recalled}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  High-gamma response (as latency) in 100 ms windows, 0--1000 ms,
  z-scored within session; per subject × lobe (≥ 5 electrodes) × window:
  L2 logistic regression (C = 7.2e-4, Ezzyat et al.~2017), balanced
  class weights, leave-one-session-out (single-session subjects:
  leave-one-list-out); AUC of pooled held-out predictions.
\item
  Across subjects: one-sided t of AUC \textgreater{} 0.5, BH-FDR over
  lobe × window; permutation chance (\textasciitilde0.49) reported only.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Reliability}\label{reliability}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Subjects' sessions split at random into two halves (≥ 2 sessions per
  half; riley-thesis rules); per half, d (power) or node strength
  (synchrony) averaged per anatomical region; test-retest = Pearson r
  across the subject's regions between halves.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Statistics}\label{statistics}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Sessions averaged per electrode, electrodes per subject × region (≥ 1
  electrode); one-sample t vs 0 across subjects; BH-FDR within each
  figure / band; ≥ 30 subjects per region. (Holmes \& Friston 1998;
  Benjamini \& Hochberg 1995; Seabold \& Perktold 2010)
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Validation (simulations, real montages and events, signal
replaced)}\label{validation-simulations-real-montages-and-events-signal-replaced}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Power: planted high-gamma gain recovered (≈ identity without noise), 0
  elsewhere.
\item
  ciPLV: lagged alpha coupling recovered monotonically; 0 for null,
  zero-lag leak, envelope plant; planted 400--800 ms coupling found only
  at 400--800 ms.
\item
  AEC-c: planted shared envelope recovered (0.16 → 0.39 → 0.59 with
  depth); non-target pairs 0; zero-lag leak rejected; timing correct.
\item
  Spectrum: 60 Hz notch ringing at clip edges (resolved with the 500 ms
  buffer; 64 Hz bin now unbiased).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Limitations / discussion}\label{limitations-discussion}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Word-off baseline starts \textasciitilde80-410 ms after the previous
  word's offset: possible offset response / anticipation in the
  baseline; the blank-length split (Results 4) cannot separate the two.
\item
  No ERP subtraction: evoked activity contributes to power and
  synchrony, especially occipital.
\item
  Neighbour-transferred labels (\textasciitilde11\% / 21\% disagreement
  in LOSO).
\item
  High-gamma phase metrics avoided (broadband HFA has no meaningful
  phase); AEC-c null is consistent with high gamma being local and
  asynchronous (Solomon et al.~2017, 2019).
\item
  Multiple comparisons corrected within figures, not across.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Stimulus-locked activity in
connectivity}\label{stimulus-locked-activity-in-connectivity}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Subtracting the average ERP leaves a residual because amplitude and
  latency vary across trials: Truccolo et al.~2002; Wang, Chen \& Ding
  2008; Knuth et al.~2006.
\item
  Evoked and induced activity can't be cleanly separated: David et
  al.~2006; Shah et al.~2004. This supports the PI's position.
\item
  Is alpha phase reset? For: Makeig et al.~2002; Hanslmayr et al.~2007.
  Against: Mazaheri \& Jensen 2006. Hard to tell apart: Yeung et
  al.~2004; Sauseng et al.~2007.
\item
  Task-evoked activity inflates connectivity: Cole et al.~2019; van
  Diepen \& Mazaheri 2018; Kramer et al.~2008 (sharp transients).
\item
  Alternatives to subtraction:

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Matched condition contrast with a label-shuffle null: Solomon et
    al.~2017; Maris et al.~2007.
  \item
    Trial-shuffled (shift-predictor) surrogates: Perkel et al.~1967;
    Aertsen et al.~1989; Brody 1999.
  \item
    Connectivity aligned to internal HFA peaks: Dede et al.~2025.
  \item
    Phase consistency on the same data: Colyer \& Kahana 2025.
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
