\author{Juee Naik, (Riley DeHaan), Michael Kahana}

\newcommand{\abstracttext}{Intracranial studies of free recall mostly ask how activity during 
word presentation predicts later memory, while the response to the word itself has received less attention. 
Work on visual word processing with scalp EEG, MEG and fMRI describes a progression from visual to 
language and memory regions, but these methods cannot resolve local activity in both space and time.
Previous intracranial studies have typically recorded from tens of patients with coverage concentrated 
in the ventral visual stream. Here we map the word-onset response across the brain in 362 neurosurgical 
patients performing delayed free recall, comparing each word with the blank screen that preceded it. 
The response was largest in occipital cortex, where alpha (8--13 Hz) power fell and high-gamma 
(70--150 Hz) power rose, and the same signature of engagement appeared, more weakly, in limbic 
and hippocampal regions. Frontal cortex instead showed increased low-frequency and decreased beta power,
 consistent with suppression. High-gamma responses began in lateral occipital cortex (${\sim}$120 ms) 
 and moved forward, reaching orbitofrontal cortex last (${\sim}$300 ms), while alpha synchrony with 
 the rest of the brain increased most in occipital cortex. The occipital response was larger when 
 the word was more expected, after longer waits, and the response weakly predicted later recall, 
 from ${\sim}$200 ms onward and most persistently in frontal cortex. Together, these results show how 
 the response to a word unfolds across the brain, from early visual engagement to later frontal 
 involvement, and provide a baseline for interpreting cognitive effects measured during word presentation.}

\section*{Introduction}\label{introduction}

\subsection*{The word-onset response in free recall}\label{intro-word-onset}

Most intracranial EEG (iEEG) studies of free recall contrast words that are later recalled with words 
that are forgotten (the subsequent memory effect, SME), or study the retrieval period. 
As both conditions contain a word, this contrast measures how responses differ with later memory, 
but cancels out the response to the word itself. Only a few studies have examined the word-onset response directly. \citet{Kucewicz_2019} showed that high-gamma (HG) activity after word onset moves from visual cortex to the medial temporal lobe (MTL) and then to prefrontal cortex, and \citet{Marks_2021} found that theta, alpha/beta and gamma activity each spread across regions on their own timelines during word encoding.

\subsection*{High gamma and alpha as markers of cortical engagement (cut/reduce?)}\label{intro-hg-alpha}

Broadband HG (70--150 Hz) is widely used as a measure of local population spiking \citep{Manning_2009,Ray_2011}. 
At stimulus onset, alpha and beta power decrease \citep[event-related desynchronization;][]{Pfurtscheller_1999}; 
together, an alpha decrease and an HG increase mark a region engaged by a stimulus. 
Conversely, alpha increases in task-irrelevant regions are thought to mean suppression \citep{Jensen_2010}, 
so the same two measures can separate regions that a stimulus engages from regions it suppresses.

\subsection*{Timing of visual word processing (most imp.)}\label{intro-timing}

Reading a word progresses in stages: visual features are extracted within about 100 ms \citep{Thorpe_1996}, 
the letter string is recognised as a word at about 200 ms \citep{Cohen_2000}, its sound is activated 
automatically \citep{van_Orden_1987}, and its meaning is integrated with context by about 400 ms 
\citep{Kutas_1980}. These stages imply a reading network that runs from occipital cortex through left 
fusiform gyrus, which contains the visual word form area \citep[VWFA;][]{Cohen_2000,Dehaene_2011}, to 
temporal, supramarginal and inferior frontal cortex \citep{Price_2012,Taylor_2013}, with posterior 
responses to letter strings preceding more anterior responses to lexical status \citep{Nobre_1994,Allison_1994}. 
Much of this picture comes from scalp EEG, MEG, and fMRI, which resolve the order in which these stages 
occur but not precisely when and where, while iEEG records local activity with both 
millisecond and millimetre precision \citep{Parvizi_2018}. However, most intracranial studies of reading 
record from tens of patients with sparse, clinically determined coverage and focus on the ventral stream, 
and so the order in which regions across the whole brain respond to a word has not been measured within one 
large dataset.

\subsection*{Synchrony, expectation and memory}\label{intro-sync-expect-memory}

Beyond local activity, word onset may change how regions synchronize. Alpha phase synchrony is thought 
to coordinate distant regions \citep{Palva_2007}, and in free recall, low-frequency synchrony between regions 
increases during successful encoding and retrieval \citep{Solomon_2017,Rao_2025}. The response may also 
depend on when the word is expected: the blank before each word varies in length, and the longer it lasts, 
the more expected the word becomes \citep{Nobre_2017}. Finally, spectral features recorded while a word 
is studied predict whether it will be recalled \citep{Ezzyat_2017}, and comparing recalled with forgotten 
words region by region, HFA predicting later recall appears first in visual, fusiform and medial 
temporal regions and later in left inferior frontal, posterior parietal and ventrolateral temporal 
cortex \citep{Burke_2014a}. These approaches either pool features across the brain and the whole word 
period, or test each region and time point for a difference in mean activity. How well each region's 
single-trial response predicts recall, and when that prediction arises, has not been measured with 
classifiers.

\subsection*{The present study (change/delete heading)}\label{intro-present}

Here we characterize the word-onset response in 362 neurosurgical patients performing delayed free recall, 
contrasting each word with the blank screen before it. We ask (1) which regions respond, and in what order;
(2) how regions synchronize, using phase and envelope connectivity; (3) whether the response depends 
on how long the subject waited for the word; (4) whether, where and when single-trial responses 
predict later recall; and (5) whether each subject's response is reliable across sessions.

\section*{Methods}\label{methods}

\subsection*{Data and cohort}\label{data-and-cohort}
We analyzed intracranial EEG (iEEG) recordings from 362 patients with pharmacoresistant epilepsy (x males, y females) who completed a delayed free-recall task while implanted with electrodes for clinical seizure monitoring. On each trial, subjects studied a list of words, completed an arithmetic distractor task (20--25 s of problems of the form ``$A + B + C = ??$'', with $A$, $B$ and $C$ drawn from the integers 1--9), and then recalled aloud as many words as they could during a 30 s retrieval period (45 s in a variant of the task). Lists were drawn from a pool of common English nouns; in catFR1, each list was built from words in a small number of semantic categories. A countdown preceded each list. Each word stayed on screen for 1600 ms and was followed by a blank screen of 750--1000 ms (800--1200 ms in the variant). A session consisted of 12 trials of 12 words (25 trials of 15 words in the variant). Data were collected as part of a multi-site collaboration at Columbia University Medical Center (New York, NY), Dartmouth-Hitchcock Medical Center (Lebanon, NH), Emory University Hospital (Atlanta, GA), the Hospital of the University of Pennsylvania (Philadelphia, PA), the Mayo Clinic (Rochester, MN), the National Institutes of Health (Bethesda, MD), the University of Colorado Anschutz Medical Campus (Aurora, CO), the University of Texas Health Science Center at San Antonio (San Antonio, TX), the University of Texas Southwestern Medical Center (Dallas, TX), and Thomas Jefferson University Hospital (Philadelphia, PA). Electrode type and placement were determined solely by clinical needs. Each site's institutional review board approved the protocol, and all patients gave informed consent. EEG was recorded at site-specific sampling rates (200--2000 Hz) and aligned to behavioral events using hardware synchronization pulses or online timing messages.

\subsection*{Events and analysis windows}\label{events}
We compared a ``word on'' window ($0$ to $-700$ ms after word onset) with a ``word off'' window ($-700$ to $-100$ ms, the blank screen before the same word), so that each pair of windows came from the same trial. The word-off window ends 100 ms before onset, and because the shortest blank is 750 ms, it starts at least 50 ms after the previous word left the screen. Both windows are 600 ms long, so spectral estimates have the same resolution and bias in each. We excluded the first word of each list, as it follows the countdown rather than a blank screen. After exclusions, X $\pm$ Y (mean $\pm$ SD across subjects) word events per session remained.

\subsection*{Preprocessing}\label{preprocessing}
Recordings were re-referenced to a bipolar montage by subtracting the signals of adjacent contacts \citep{Mercier_2022,Li_2018}. Word-on and word-off clips were loaded separately for each word, each extended by 500 ms of real (not mirrored) data on both sides to absorb filter edge artifacts. Clips were resampled to 500 Hz and filtered for line noise with 4th-order Butterworth band-stop filters ($\pm$2 Hz) at the mains frequency (60 Hz). To give every subject the same passband within the high-gamma range, we also notched the 100, 120 and 150 Hz harmonics in all recordings. The buffers were then removed before any further analysis.

We excluded bipolar pairs whose contacts were more than 20 mm apart and channels with flat signals. We excluded whole sessions with a missing or sub-500 Hz sampling rate, because after resampling to 500 Hz the 250 Hz Nyquist limit must sit well above the 70--150 Hz high-gamma band. We also excluded sessions with residual line noise, defined as a spectral peak at a non-notched mains harmonic more than five times the power at neighbouring frequencies (9 sessions). 

\subsection*{Electrode localization}\label{electrode-localization}
Electrode contacts were localized with the lab's standard pipeline. Each patient's pre-implant T1-weighted MRI was segmented and parcellated with FreeSurfer \citep{Fischl_2002,Fischl_2004,Desikan_2006}, and the post-implant CT was co-registered to it [ANTs: add Avants et al.\ 2008 to refs.bib]. Contacts were identified manually and assigned anatomical labels automatically \citep{Dykstra_2012}; a subset of labels was reviewed by neuroradiologists. Where a T2-weighted MRI was available, medial temporal lobe subregions were labelled automatically \citep{Yushkevich_2014}. Each bipolar pair took the label at the midpoint of its two contacts, from volumetric labels for depth electrodes and surface labels for grid and strip electrodes \citep{Azarion_2014}. For pairs whose label was missing from the standard database, we recovered it from the imaging pipeline's own label files: either the pair's own midpoint label or, when the two contacts skipped a contact, that intermediate contact's label. We grouped labels into 12 regions of interest: frontal, temporal, parietal, occipital and limbic cortex (excluding the hippocampus), plus the hippocampus, each split by hemisphere \citep{Burke_2013}. Supplementary Table (ADD) lists the mapping from anatomical labels to regions.

\subsection*{Power}\label{power}
Alpha (8--13 Hz) and 5--150 Hz spectrum: multitaper, 2 Hz bandwidth,
600 ms windows; spectrum in 11 log-spaced bins (50, 60, 100, 120 Hz
$\pm$ 2 Hz excluded). \citep{Thomson_1982,Slepian_1978,Percival_1993,Prerau_2017}
High gamma (70--150 Hz): Hilbert in 8 × 10 Hz sub-bands, each divided
by its word-off mean, averaged (broadband, every frequency weighted
equally), as HFA is better described as a broadband signal than as an oscillation \citep{Burke_2015a}. \citep{Edwards_2009,Bruns_2004,Manning_2009,Ray_2011} \textbf{(check Edwards et al.\ 2009)}
Per electrode: Cohen's d of per-trial power, word on vs word off.
\citep{Cohen_2013,Lakens_2013}

\subsection*{High-gamma latency}\label{high-gamma-latency}

  Responsive electrodes: per-trial high-gamma power word on
  \textgreater{} word off (one-tailed Welch t test, BH-FDR within
  session): 4950 of 122,339 electrode-sessions, 299 subjects. \citep{WELCH_1947,Benjamini_1995}; on selecting electrodes and measuring them in the same data, see \citet{Kriegeskorte_2009}

  Latency: trial-mean envelope minus word-off mean, 30 ms smoothing,
  first time it reaches half its peak within 0--800 ms (half-maximum).
  \citep{Kiesel_2007,Liesefeld_2018}; on filter smearing of onsets, see \citet{VanRullen_2011}

  Subject median per region → mean ± SEM across subjects; 12 ROIs and
  subregions (hemispheres pooled).

\begin{itemize}

  MNE-Connectivity (Zenodo))
\item
  High gamma: AEC-c (70--110 Hz band-pass + Hilbert,

  (Bedrosian). \citep{Hipp_2012,Bedrosian_1963}
\item
  Pairs sharing a contact excluded. \citep{Mercier_2017} Per electrode:

\item
  Synchrony is treated as a secondary analysis: an evoked response shared across regions can raise phase and envelope coupling without any interaction between them (see Discussion).
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Time-resolved network
(epochs)}\label{time-resolved-network-epochs}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  9 overlapping 200 ms epochs every 100 ms, 0--1000 ms (stops before the
  word's offset response at 1600 ms), each minus the mean of three 200
  ms word-off epochs.
\item
  Region pairs with $\geq$ 100 subjects. Hub (Rao): a region's mean change
  over its connections; t across subjects; two-stage FDR
  (Benjamini--Krieger--Yekutieli) over region × epoch. \citep{Benjamini_2006,Rubinov_2010}
\item
  Edges: each significant hub's 5 strongest connections (\textbar mean
  change\textbar), either sign; width $\propto$ \textbar t\textbar{} (\citealp{Solomon_2017} style).
\end{itemize}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist

  Responsive electrodes (as latency); trials split at the session's

  response after long minus short waits, both relative to the

\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Recall prediction (encoding contrast: recalled vs not
recalled)}\label{recall-prediction-encoding-contrast-recalled-vs-not-recalled}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  High-gamma response (as latency) in 100 ms windows, 0--1000 ms,
  z-scored within session; per subject × lobe ($\geq$ 5 electrodes) × window:
  L2 logistic regression \citep[C = 7.2e-4,][]{Ezzyat_2017}, balanced
  class weights, leave-one-session-out (single-session subjects:
  leave-one-list-out); AUC of pooled held-out predictions.
\item
  Across subjects: one-sided t of AUC \textgreater{} 0.5, BH-FDR over
  lobe × window; permutation chance (\textasciitilde0.49) reported only.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Reliability}\label{reliability}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Subjects' sessions split at random into two halves ($\geq$ 2 sessions per
  half; riley-thesis rules); per half, d (power) or node strength
  (synchrony) averaged per anatomical region; test-retest = Pearson r
  across the subject's regions between halves.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Statistics}\label{statistics}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Sessions averaged per electrode, electrodes per subject × region ($\geq$ 1
  electrode); one-sample t vs 0 across subjects; BH-FDR within each
  figure / band; $\geq$ 30 subjects per region. \citep{Holmes_1998,Benjamini_1995,Seabold_2010}
\end{itemize}

\section*{Discussion}\label{discussion}

\subsection*{Power}\label{disc-power}

The occipital alpha decrease and HG increase match the activation response first described in sensorimotor cortex \citep{Crone_1998a,Crone_1998b} and in visual cortex \citep{Lachaux_2005}, including for pseudowords and consonant strings, where the electrodes selective in each band overlapped little \citep{Vidal_2010}. The same low-frequency decrease and HFA increase marks successful encoding \citep{Sederberg_2003,Long_2014}, and this broadband tilt also appears outside memory, during arithmetic \citep{Randazzo_2019}, where it predicts response speed, mainly in frontal regions \citep{DeHaan_2025}. The frontal low-frequency increase and beta decrease, without a frontal HG increase, fit active suppression of cortex not needed for the task \citep{Jensen_2010}; HG similarly decreases during tasks in default-mode regions \citep{Ossand_n_2011}.

\subsection*{Timing}\label{disc-timing}

Our latencies agree with smaller iEEG studies. Gamma activity begins at about 150 ms in basal temporal cortex, reaches the VWFA at about 200 ms, and reaches superior temporal and Broca's areas after 400 ms \citep{Mainy_2007}; word-selective ventral temporal electrodes respond at about 150 ms \citep{Kadipasaoglu_2016}; and the effects of word properties move anteriorly along the ventral stream, with feedback to posterior regions at 250--500 ms \citep{Woolnough_2020}. Beyond the ventral stream, MEG shows responses proceeding from temporal cortex to Broca's area and finally to orbitofrontal cortex at about 370 ms \citep{Halgren_2002}, the same order as ours with orbitofrontal cortex last, and MTL neurons respond in the same order as our MTL regions but later, at about 270--400 ms \citep{Mormann_2008}, as expected for selective single-neuron responses compared with population HG. The sharp early and broad late responses we observe are consistent with feedforward input followed by feedback \citep{Halgren_2006,Ghuman_2014}.

\subsection*{Synchrony}\label{disc-synchrony}

Alpha power can fall while alpha synchrony between regions rises \citep{Hanslmayr_2016}, the combination we observe in occipital cortex. The absence of HG envelope coupling is consistent with HFA being local and asynchronous: regions with gamma increases during successful encoding lose global synchrony \citep{Burke_2013}. Synchrony measured at stimulus onset is, however, harder to interpret than power: an evoked response shared across regions can raise phase and envelope coupling without any interaction between them, and subtracting the average evoked response does not fully remove it, because its amplitude and latency vary across trials \citep{Truccolo_2002,David_2006}.

\subsection*{Expectation}\label{disc-expectation}

Temporally expected events evoke larger visual responses: in monkey V1, gamma increases and alpha decreases when a stimulus is expected \citep{Lima_2011}, and expectation speeds and enhances visual processing in humans \citep{Doherty_2005}. The larger occipital response after longer waits fits this account. Because long- and short-wait trials were measured against the same trial-averaged baseline, the effect cannot come from a raised baseline after short waits; an alternative is that the visual response recovers less from the previous word when the interval is short.

\subsection*{Recall prediction}\label{disc-recall}

Recall prediction appeared only after the early visual response, briefly in occipital cortex and persistently in frontal cortex, in the same direction as the early posterior and late frontal HFA effects found by comparing recalled with forgotten words \citep{Burke_2014a}. These classifiers may partly track general fluctuations in brain state rather than encoding of the item \citep{Weidemann_2021}.

\subsection*{Limitations}\label{limitations-discussion}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Word-off baseline starts \textasciitilde80-410 ms after the previous
  word's offset: possible offset response / anticipation in the
  baseline; the blank-length analysis (Results 4) uses one
  trial-averaged baseline and is not affected by it.
\item
  No ERP subtraction: evoked activity contributes to power and
  synchrony, especially occipital.
\item
  Neighbour-transferred labels (\textasciitilde11\% / 21\% disagreement
  in LOSO) only for the 13\% of unlabelled depth pairs the imaging-pipeline label files
  lack.
\item
  High-gamma phase metrics avoided (broadband HFA has no meaningful
  phase); AEC-c null is consistent with high gamma being local and
  asynchronous \citep{Solomon_2017,Solomon_2019a}.
\item
  Multiple comparisons corrected within figures, not across.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Stimulus-locked activity in
connectivity (cut?)}\label{stimulus-locked-activity-in-connectivity}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Subtracting the average ERP leaves a residual because amplitude and
  latency vary across trials: \citealp{Truccolo_2002,Wang_2008,Knuth_2006}.
\item
  Evoked and induced activity can't be cleanly separated: \citealp{David_2006,Shah_2004}. Supports Mike's / Riley's position.
\item
  Is alpha phase reset? For: \citealp{Makeig_2002,Hanslmayr_2007}.
  Against: \citealp{Mazaheri_2006}. Hard to tell apart: \citealp{Yeung_2004,Sauseng_2007}
\item
  Task-evoked activity inflates connectivity: \citealp{Cole_2019,van_Diepen_2018,Kramer_2008} 
\item
  Alternatives to subtraction:

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Matched condition contrast with a label-shuffle null: \citealp{Solomon_2017,Maris_2007a}.
  \item
    Trial-shuffled (shift-predictor) surrogates: \citealp{Perkel_1967,Aertsen_1989,Brody_1999b}.
  \item
    Connectivity aligned to internal HFA peaks: \citealp{Dede_2025}.
  \item
    Phase consistency on the same data: \citealp{Colyer_2025}.
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
